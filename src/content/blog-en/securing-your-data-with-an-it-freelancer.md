Working with an IT freelancer is often the ideal way to move fast on your tech projects without adding to your payroll. But here's the thing: as soon as an external contractor accesses your systems, your source code or your customer databases, data security becomes a central issue. Because a leak, an oversight or poorly managed access can cost a lot, a great deal, both in reputation and in GDPR compliance.

So how do you collaborate with peace of mind with an IT freelancer while protecting your sensitive information? Between contract clauses, technical tools and good organisational practices, we explain how to put the right safeguards in place without hampering the effectiveness of your collaboration.

## Why data security is critical with an IT freelancer

Unlike a permanent staff member, an IT freelancer works on a one-off basis, often remotely, sometimes from the other side of the world. They juggle several clients, use their own tools, and aren't always subject to the same internal processes as your teams. The result: the risks multiply.

### The most common risks

When you open up your servers or development environments to an external contractor, several scenarios can go wrong. A freelancer can unintentionally expose data via an unsecured computer, public WiFi or a badly configured personal cloud. Some also retain access after the engagement ends, through simple oversight or a lack of process on the client side.

Then there's the question of freelance confidentiality: your contractor works for other companies, sometimes competitors. Without a solid IT freelance NDA, nothing theoretically stops them from reusing bits of code, concepts or strategic information elsewhere.

Finally, in the event of a cyberattack targeting the freelancer themselves, your systems can become an entry point if access hasn't been properly compartmentalised.

### What the law says (and what it costs you)

The GDPR makes no distinction between staff and freelancers: as soon as someone processes personal data on your behalf, they're considered a processor in the legal sense. You remain responsible for protecting that data. In the event of a leak, it's your company that gets the fine, not the freelancer.

In concrete terms, what does that mean? That you must be able to prove you took all the necessary measures to govern access to the data: contract, confidentiality clause, access management, traceability. Otherwise, the *CNIL* (France's data protection authority) can fine you up to 4% of your annual worldwide turnover. Not exactly the kind of budget line you plan for.

## The contractual fundamentals to protect your information

Before giving an IT freelancer any access at all, the contract must set the rules of the game. No need for a 50-page legal tome, but some clauses are non-negotiable.

### The confidentiality clause (NDA)

The NDA, or non-disclosure agreement, is the foundation. It stipulates that any information exchanged as part of the engagement remains strictly confidential, during and after the collaboration. The freelancer commits not to disclose anything, nor to reuse your data, code or processes elsewhere.

A good NDA also specifies the duration of confidentiality (often 3 to 5 years after the end of the engagement), any exceptions (information already public, for example), and the penalties for breach. Ideally, have it signed before you even send the slightest detailed brief.

### The intellectual property clause

Who owns the code developed by the freelancer? By default, under French law, they do. Unless your contract explicitly provides for an assignment of rights. This clause must specify that all deliverables, developments, documentation and creations produced as part of the engagement belong to you in full.

This is crucial, especially if you plan to sell your solution, raise funds or simply evolve the project with another team later on.

### Security and GDPR compliance obligations

Your contract must explicitly mention the freelancer's data protection obligations. For example: encryption of data in transit and at rest, use of GDPR-compliant tools, immediate notification in the event of a security incident, destruction of data after the engagement.

If the freelancer processes personal data (customer emails, HR information, etc.), they must sign a GDPR processing agreement (DPA - Data Processing Agreement) detailing their responsibilities. It's a legal requirement, not an option.

## Good technical practices to limit risk

The contract is a good start. But on the technical side, you also need to lock down access and reduce the attack surface. Here's how to do it without making your freelancer's life complicated.

### The principle of least privilege

Grant only the access strictly necessary to carry out the engagement. A developer working on an API doesn't need access to the full customer database, or to the production servers. Create dedicated user accounts, with permissions limited to the scope of work.

On GitHub, GitLab or Bitbucket, don't systematically grant admin rights. Read-write access to the relevant repositories is more than enough in most cases. Same logic for your cloud environments (AWS, Azure, GCP): use IAM roles to compartmentalise access.

### Access management and strong authentication

Enable two-factor authentication (2FA) on all critical tools: code platforms, cloud platforms, project management tools, VPN. It's the simplest and most effective measure against unauthorised access.

Also use a shared password manager (1Password, Bitwarden, LastPass) to pass on credentials without sending them in plain text by email or Slack. And above all, change all passwords once the engagement is over.

### Isolated environments and VPN

If possible, have your freelancers work on development or staging environments isolated from production. This limits the risk in the event of an error or compromise. Production data should never be directly accessible, except in absolutely necessary and logged exceptions.

For remote access, require the use of a company VPN rather than a direct connection. This lets you log connections, filter authorised IPs and cut off access instantly if needed.

### Encryption and secure transfer

All data exchanged with the freelancer must be encrypted. For sensitive files, use solutions such as Tresorit, Sync.com or even WeTransfer in encrypted mode (with a password). Avoid unprotected email attachments.

If the freelancer needs to access databases, require SSL/TLS connections. And if data is stored temporarily on their side (which should be avoided as far as possible), require full-disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault, LUKS).

## Organising the collaboration to stay in control

Beyond the contractual and technical aspects, the way you organise the day-to-day collaboration has a huge impact on information security.

### Onboarding and security training

From the start of the engagement, take 30 minutes to brief the freelancer on your security requirements. Explain the tools to use, the prohibited practices (storage on a personal Dropbox, password sharing, etc.), and the processes to follow in case of doubt or an incident.

Share a good practices document: how to manage access, where to store files, how to report a problem. The clearer it is from the outset, the fewer nasty surprises you'll have.

### Traceability and auditing of actions

Enable logs on all critical systems. You need to be able to know who did what, when and from which IP. On GitHub, AWS, your CRM or your ERP, this data is often available natively. Check it regularly, especially if you notice suspicious behaviour.

Some tools such as Datadog, Splunk or even CloudWatch can automate anomaly detection: logins from an unusual country, mass data downloads, changes to critical permissions.

### Regular access reviews

Don't leave active access lying around indefinitely. Every month, review the external accounts that are still active: are they still needed? Is the freelancer still on the engagement? If not, deactivate them immediately.

Ideally, automate account deactivation after a period of inactivity (for example 30 days without a login) or on the planned contract end date.

### Structured offboarding

At the end of the engagement, apply a strict checklist:

-   Revocation of all access (VPN, cloud, tools, GitHub, etc.)
-   Change of shared passwords
-   Recovery or destruction of data stored on the freelancer's side
-   Export of activity logs for archiving
-   Written confirmation of the return/destruction of data

Never rely on goodwill alone. Even the best freelancer can forget an open access or a file on their hard drive. It's better to make it systematic.

## Choosing the right IT freelancer: reassuring signals

Not all freelancers are equal when it comes to security rigour. Some signals can help you spot those who take the subject seriously.

### Certifications and awareness

A freelancer who holds certifications such as ISO 27001, CISSP, CEH or even simple GDPR training shows they've invested time in security topics. It's not an absolute guarantee, but it's a good starting point.

Likewise, if they spontaneously offer to sign an NDA, use a password manager or encrypt their communications, you know you're dealing with someone serious.

### References and reputation

Ask for client references, especially in your sector if you handle sensitive data (healthcare, finance, HR). A freelancer used to working for regulated companies already knows the compliance requirements and security standards.

Also look at their LinkedIn profile, their recommendations, and possibly their website or portfolio. Be wary of profiles that are too vague, with no verifiable history or with inconsistencies.

### Transparency about tools and methods

A good IT freelancer should be able to explain how they secure their workstation: antivirus, firewall, VPN, encryption, backups. If they use a personal Mac or PC to work on your sensitive projects without any security measures, that's not a good sign.

Likewise, they should accept your technical requirements without a murmur (2FA, isolated environments, mandatory VPN). If they find it "too complicated" or "pointless", move on.

## FAQ: your questions about security with IT freelancers

### How can I check that a freelancer follows the security rules day to day?

Trust is good, verification is better. Use access logs to check where connections come from, that authorised tools are being used and that there are no suspicious downloads. Also hold regular check-ins where you can ask about their practices. If something seems unclear, dig deeper. A serious freelancer will have no trouble explaining how they work.

### Should you always have an IT freelancer sign an NDA, even for short engagements?

Yes, absolutely. Even on a two-day engagement, the freelancer may have access to strategic information: technical architecture, product roadmap, customer data, security vulnerabilities. An NDA takes 5 minutes to sign and protects you legally. It's a reflex to have from the very first exchange, even at the briefing stage.

### What should you do if a freelancer refuses to sign a confidentiality clause?

If a freelancer refuses to sign an NDA or a confidentiality clause, that's a huge red flag. Either they don't understand what's at stake (which is worrying for an IT professional), or they have reasons to want to keep a free hand over your data or your code. In both cases, it's better to move on to the next candidate. There are thousands of skilled freelancers who accept these standard safeguards without any problem.

### How do I manage access for a freelancer who works for several of my competitors?

It's a delicate but common situation, especially in technical niches. The solution is a reinforced NDA that explicitly prohibits any reuse of code, concepts or information with other clients. Also technically restrict access to the strict minimum, and avoid sharing your overall strategy if it isn't essential to their engagement. Finally, favour deliverables in source code rather than packaged solutions that could be reused elsewhere.

### How long should a freelancer's activity logs be kept after the engagement?

The GDPR requires personal data to be kept only for as long as strictly necessary, but for access logs to your systems, the CNIL's recommendation is generally 6 months to 1 year. In practice, keep them at least until the end of the contractual warranty period (often 3 to 6 months after delivery), or even longer if you anticipate possible litigation or a security audit. Document your retention policy and apply it consistently.

## Conclusion: security and agility aren't opposites

Working with an IT freelancer means gaining flexibility, sharp expertise and speed of execution. But that agility must never come at the expense of data security. Between the contractual framework, the technical measures and rigorous organisation of the collaboration, you can perfectly well secure your sensitive information without hampering your contractors' effectiveness.

The key is to anticipate. Never start an engagement without an NDA, without clear access management and without a defined offboarding process. Treat your freelancers as trusted partners, but verify all the same. Because when it comes to cybersecurity and GDPR compliance, prevention is better than paying six-figure fines.
