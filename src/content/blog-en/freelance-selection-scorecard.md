**Hiring a freelancer** can sometimes happen in a rush: finding the right person, validating cultural fit, securing the budget, framing the deliverables… and doing it fast. Between LinkedIn profiles, specialist platforms and friends' recommendations, it's easy to scatter your efforts and lose sight of what matters: your short-term business objective.

The most pragmatic solution is to formalise a clear, short, actionable HR scorecard that aligns your criteria before you even make first contact. This guide shows you how to build and use that scorecard in under 30 minutes, whether you're assessing a solo freelancer or even ready-formed [**freelance studios**](/en/studios-and-collectives) through [**digitags.work**](/en).

## Why a scorecard speeds up hiring

The first virtue of a scorecard is clarity. Defining the criteria before opening a single profile avoids methodological wandering and endless debates at the end of interviews. You set a scope, expectations and minimum thresholds; everything that follows is just verification. This clarity reassures your team, but it also reassures the freelancer, who understands exactly what they're getting into and what is expected of them.

The second virtue is objectivity. Interviews often leave contradictory impressions: "good feeling", "very creative", "a bit expensive". A scorecard turns those impressions into measurable elements. You rate a technical skill on tangible examples, you illustrate a **soft skill** with an observed behaviour, you link added value to a specific proposal. In the end, the score speaks for itself, and the decision becomes defensible.

Finally, the scorecard brings traceability. It goes with you from the brief to onboarding and remains the reference for your reviews. It serves to validate the engagement, to objectify performance, and even to document a clean exit if priorities change. In short, it's your safeguard in a process that speed sometimes makes fragile.

## The six criteria that make the difference

Start with technical expertise. Here, forget the endless lists of buzzwords and focus on the two or three critical skills that determine delivery. If you're redesigning a landing page, the requirements will cover Figma, clean front-end build, web performance and an understanding of the UX patterns that convert. If you're restructuring a [Google Ads](/en/roles/freelance-google-ads-expert) account, you'll look at segmentation logic, the use of conversion signals and the ability to test properly. The idea is simple: validate that the person masters the professional moves that matter to you, not that they know the whole alphabet of tools.

Next, sector experience. A freelancer who has already seen average basket values close to yours, sales cycles just as long, or similar regulatory constraints starts with a head start. They know where the pitfalls lie, and they propose realistic paths. It isn't an absolute requirement, but it's a speed multiplier.

The third pillar is **soft skills**. You're not just buying deliverables, you're buying a collaboration. Communication, autonomy, the ability to simplify a technical subject, the art of feedback and active listening say a lot about the future of the relationship. A freelancer who documents their choices, who knows how to say "no" when needed and who proposes well-argued alternatives will save you weeks.

Fourth axis: availability and meeting deadlines. The best profile is useless if their calendar is full when yours is on fire. Clarify time slots, a realistic weekly workload, attendance at team rituals and how the unexpected is handled.

Fifth criterion: budget and day rate. Rather than thinking "expensive/cheap", relate the rate to the expected impact and the timeline. A higher day rate on a well-framed scope delivered in two weeks can prove more economical than a low day rate spread over endless back-and-forth. Above all, be transparent about your constraints to avoid misunderstandings.

Finally, added value. This is the share of intelligence that goes beyond execution: the idea you hadn't seen, the technical shortcut that saves a week of development, the measurement recommendation that sheds light on a marketing debate. Ask for concrete examples, post-mortems, verifiable "before/after" cases. This is often where the final preference is decided, when scores are equal.

## A simple, readable, decision-oriented weighting

You don't need a complex mathematical model. In most cases, a balance such as 30% expertise, 20% sector experience, 20% **soft skills**, 10% availability, 10% budget and 10% added value works very well.

Score out of ten, back up your scores with facts and calculate the weighted average.

If you get, for example, 8 for expertise, 7 for experience, 9 for **soft skills**, 8 for availability, 7 for budget and 9 for added value, you arrive at an overall score of around 8.1/10.

Below 7, you investigate; above 8, you can move forward with confidence. This framework avoids corridor debates and forces you to decide.

## FAQ – Scorecard & freelance hiring

### Isn't a scorecard too rigid to assess creative profiles?

On the contrary, it sets a framework that frees originality. By setting criteria such as "added value" and "clarity of approach", you measure the ability to surprise usefully, not just to make things look nice. Creativity becomes an observable skill, linked to impact.

### What if a candidate ticks almost every box except one critical one?

Set an overall threshold (for example 7/10) and identify the non-negotiable criteria. If the weakness concerns a vital element — security, compliance, a core skill — you're better off looking for another option. If it's peripheral, frame an upskilling plan and a quick review clause.

### Can you use the scorecard after onboarding?

Yes, and it's even recommended. It becomes the common thread of your reviews: what has been delivered, what is blocked, what changes in the next sprint. It structures feedback in both directions and informs budget allocation.
