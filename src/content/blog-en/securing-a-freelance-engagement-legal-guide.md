Engaging a freelancer often means flexibility, sharp expertise and time saved. But when you start bringing in freelancers, one question keeps coming up: how do you make sure everything is watertight from a legal standpoint? Because a contractual misunderstanding, a vague clause or a poorly defined status can quickly turn a promising collaboration into an administrative headache. And nobody wants to receive a reassessment from the *URSSAF* (France's social contributions collection agency) or find themselves caught up in a dispute with a contractor they thought was independent.

Legally securing a freelance engagement isn't optional, it's a professional reflex. This article gives you the keys to bring in freelancers with peace of mind, draft a solid freelance contract and avoid costly pitfalls.

Unlike a permanent contract (CDI), where the French Labour Code governs almost everything, the relationship with a freelancer rests on a commercial contract. In other words, you set the rules of the game. And if those rules are shaky, you expose yourself to several major risks.

### The spectre of reclassification as salaried work

This is the nightmare of many companies. If the URSSAF or a court considers that your freelancer was in fact working as a disguised salaried worker, you risk *requalification* (reclassification of the contract as salaried work). In practice, this means retroactively paying social contributions, paid leave, and potentially damages. The reclassification criteria are clear: a relationship of subordination, imposed working hours, provision of work tools, lack of autonomy. If your freelancer looks like an employee, they'll be treated as one.

### Intellectual property: who owns what?

Imagine you engage a freelance designer to overhaul your visual identity. Without an assignment of rights clause in the contract, they remain the owner of their creations. You only have a limited right of use. The result: it's impossible to fully exploit the work you commissioned without renegotiating, sometimes at a steep price. This happens more often than you'd think, especially in the creative and tech sectors.

### Confidentiality and non-compete clauses

A freelancer can work for several clients at the same time. If you give them access to strategic information (customer database, product roadmap, proprietary algorithms), you need to govern how it's used. A well-drafted confidentiality clause protects your intangible assets. As for non-compete clauses, they're tricky with an independent contractor, but can be negotiated in certain specific cases.

## The fundamentals of a rock-solid freelance contract

A good freelance contract is a balanced contract. It protects the company without stifling the contractor. Here are the essential elements to include from the very first page.

### The purpose of the engagement and expected deliverables

Be precise. "Develop a website" isn't enough. Specify the expected features, the technologies used, the delivery deadlines and the acceptance criteria. The more detailed it is, the less ambiguity there will be. This clarity protects both parties: you know what you're buying, the freelancer knows what's expected of them.

### Duration and renewal terms

Set a fixed term, with a start date and an end date. If the engagement can be renewed, specify the conditions. Be careful not to chain too many renewals over a long period without a break, as this could be interpreted as a disguised permanent contract.

### Fees and payment terms

Fixed price or day rate: choose the model suited to the engagement. Specify the invoicing terms: frequency, payment deadline (generally 30 days), late payment penalties. A freelancer who isn't paid on time can suspend their services or start debt recovery proceedings.

### Liability and insurance clauses

A freelancer must be covered by professional liability insurance (*RC Pro*), especially on higher-risk engagements (consulting, development, construction). Ask for a certificate before the start. On the company side, specify the limits of liability in the event of delays, non-conformity or disputes.

### Early termination

Nobody likes to think about it at signing time, but planning the exit conditions is essential. Notice period, any compensation, return of access and documents: everything must be written down in black and white. A badly handled termination can cost a lot of time and energy.

## Distinguishing a service relationship from subordination

This is THE number-one point of vigilance when **bringing in freelancers**. The line between a commercial collaboration and a salaried relationship can be thin, and URSSAF inspectors know it by heart.

### The autonomy criteria to respect

A freelancer is an independent professional. They choose their hours, their place of work, their methods. You can't impose time clocks, mandatory daily meetings or a fixed desk in your offices. You're buying a result, not a presence. Leave them free to organise their work as they see fit, as long as the deliverables are there.

### Avoiding red flags

A few situations to avoid at all costs: providing a computer and phone with your logo, including the freelancer in the org chart, assigning them a direct manager, subjecting them to the same rules as your salaried staff (hours, leave, internal procedures). All these practices can be taken as evidence of disguised salaried work.

### Having several clients

A freelancer must be able to work for several companies. If you're their sole client over a long period, that's a risk factor. Admittedly, you can't control their client portfolio, but avoid creating exclusive economic dependence. It counts against you in the event of an inspection.

## Sector-specific rules to know

The **legal framework for freelancing** isn't uniform. Depending on your sector of activity, particular rules apply.

### Construction and the duty of vigilance

In construction, the client company must check that its subcontractor (freelancer or company) is up to date with its social security declarations. This is the *attestation de vigilance* (certificate of compliance with social contributions), to be requested every six months. Without it, you can be held jointly liable for your contractor's debts. A heavy constraint, but a mandatory one.

### Regulated professions

Some professions require a degree, registration with a professional body or a specific qualification (architects, chartered accountants, lawyers, medical professions). Before engaging a freelancer in these fields, check their credentials. Using an unqualified contractor can expose you to criminal liability.

### Personal data protection (GDPR)

If your freelancer accesses personal data (customers, staff, prospects), they become a processor within the meaning of the GDPR. You must sign a specific data processing agreement setting out their obligations regarding security, confidentiality and breach notification. GDPR fines can reach several million euros, so it's best to be watertight from the start.

## Checklist before signing a freelance contract

To wrap up, here's a summary checklist to tick off before any signature. Keep it close at hand: it'll save you from costly oversights.

**Before signing:**

-   Check the freelancer's legal status (*auto-entrepreneur* (France's micro-business status), *SASU*, *EURL* (single-member companies), *portage salarial* (umbrella company arrangement))
-   Ask for a *Kbis* extract (company registration certificate) or a certificate of registration
-   Require a valid professional liability (RC Pro) insurance certificate
-   Confirm registration with the self-employed social security scheme (URSSAF)
-   Precisely define the purpose of the engagement and the deliverables
-   Set clear fees and payment terms
-   Include a confidentiality clause
-   Provide for the assignment of intellectual property rights
-   Anticipate the termination conditions
-   Make sure the contractor is autonomous (no relationship of subordination)

**During the engagement:**

-   Don't impose fixed hours or a fixed place of work
-   Communicate about objectives, not methods
-   Approve deliverables according to the criteria set out in the contract
-   Pay within the agreed deadlines

**At the end of the engagement:**

-   Recover all access, data and documents
-   Formalise the close-out with an acceptance report if necessary
-   Archive the contract and invoices (10-year legal obligation)

## FAQ: legally securing a freelance engagement

### What's the difference between a service contract and an employment contract?

A service contract binds two commercial entities on a defined engagement, with no relationship of subordination. The contractor is autonomous in organising their work. An employment contract, by contrast, creates a relationship of subordination: the employer gives orders, controls how the work is done and provides the tools. Legally, it's this subordination that makes all the difference.

### Can you impose working hours on a freelancer?

No. Imposing fixed hours (such as "9am-6pm at the office") characterises a relationship of subordination and exposes you to reclassification as salaried work. You can set occasional availability slots (meetings, progress check-ins), but the freelancer remains free to organise their schedule as they see fit to reach the agreed objectives.

### What should you do if a freelancer doesn't deliver as planned?

It all depends on what's written in the **freelance contract**. If the deliverables and deadlines are specified, you can demand that the work be brought into line or trigger a penalty clause. As a last resort, terminate the contract for breach, following the agreed procedure. Without a clear contract, you'll struggle to assert your rights.

### Do you have to pay a freelancer even if the result isn't satisfactory?

If the work meets the contract's specifications, yes. If it doesn't, you can refuse to approve it and ask for corrections. Hence the importance of defining objective acceptance criteria from the start. A precise specification is your best insurance against disappointment.

### How do I protect my sensitive data when working with a freelancer?

Through a strict confidentiality clause in the contract, supplemented if necessary by a reinforced non-disclosure agreement (NDA). Also limit access to the data strictly necessary for the engagement. Finally, if you're subject to the GDPR, sign a specific data processing agreement governing the processing of personal data.
