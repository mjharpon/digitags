Bringing in a freelancer for a strategic project is a bit like choosing a driver for a rally: you don't have three months for test runs. You need to know very quickly whether the person in front of you masters their craft or is bluffing with a well-marketed CV. Choosing a freelancer demands a clear, fast, reliable method to qualify technical skills without wasting time or money.

In this article, I share the concrete techniques we use at digitags.work to evaluate a freelancer's skills in a few steps, without falling into the trap of inflated CVs or biased interviews. Whether you're an HR director, a CTO or a small-business leader, you'll see how to structure your selection process and avoid nasty surprises mid-mission.

## Why technical evaluation is crucial when choosing a freelancer

Unlike a permanent hire, where a probation period lets you correct course, a freelancer dives straight into the thick of it. No margin for error: every day counts, every deliverable impacts your project. Poorly qualifying an IT freelancer can cost you weeks of delay, blown budgets and an internal team frustrated at having to pick up the pieces.

The problem is that many decision-makers rely solely on the portfolio or LinkedIn recommendations. The result: three weeks later you discover that your "confirmed React expert" struggles with hooks, or that your "senior DevOps" can't actually automate a CI/CD pipeline. That's where a technical test comes in.

## The classic mistakes that sink your selection process

Before talking method, let's talk about the traps 80% of recruiters fall into:

**Relying on the CV alone.** Freelancers know how to sell themselves. A polished CV with all the buzzwords of the moment guarantees absolutely nothing about real operational ability. We've seen profiles claiming "10 years of Python experience" who couldn't explain the difference between a list and a tuple.

**The chit-chat interview.** You spend an hour discussing company culture, agile methodology and vision. Nice — but it tells you nothing about whether the person writes clean code. The interview must validate concrete skills, not just the vibe.

**The out-of-context test.** You send an ultra-academic "sorting algorithm" exercise when your real need is integrating a REST API into a mobile app. The test must match your business context, or it's useless.

**Leaving the tech side out.** HR or the manager filters alone, without technical validation. The result: you bring in someone who talks well but doesn't deliver. When choosing a freelance developer, the tech side must always have a say.

## The 4-step method to evaluate a freelancer quickly

Down to business. Here's the process we systematically apply to qualify a technical profile in under 48 hours, from first contact to final decision.

### Step 1: The portfolio and production-code filter

Always ask to see real code. Not a Codingame exercise, not a dusty side project on GitHub: production code, from a real client project. You want to verify several things:

**Code quality.** Is it readable? Are variables named properly? Are the comments useful, or is it just auto-generated code? A good developer writes for the team, not for the machine.

**Architecture.** Is the project structured intelligently, or is everything dumped into a single 3,000-line file? Are the patterns suited to the context, or is it over-engineering for show?

**Tests.** If there's no unit test or integration test at all, that's a major red flag. A senior freelancer knows that a project without tests is a time bomb.

If the freelancer refuses to share code or systematically invokes confidentiality, either they have nothing to show, or they worked on projects they don't really master. Either way, move on.

### Step 2: The use-case-driven technical interview

The goal here isn't to ask trick questions or play development Trivial Pursuit. You want to measure reasoning ability, understanding of business stakes and depth of expertise.

Prepare 3 to 5 questions based on real situations from your project. For example:

"We need to migrate our MySQL database to PostgreSQL with zero downtime. How would you structure that migration?" The freelancer should be able to walk through a step-by-step strategy, identify the risks and propose a rollback plan.

"We have a performance problem on our API: response times explode beyond 1,000 requests per minute. What are your first lines of investigation?" Here you're evaluating their debugging methodology, their knowledge of monitoring tools, their ability to prioritise.

"If you had to onboard a junior onto this project tomorrow, how would you go about it?" That tests professional maturity. A freelancer who knows how to pass knowledge on is a freelancer who truly understands what they're doing.

The idea isn't to wait for THE right answer. It's to see how the person reasons, asks questions, challenges your assumptions. A good profile will dig into the context before proposing a solution.

### Step 3: The technical test

The technical test remains the most effective tool for measuring real skills — provided you calibrate it intelligently. The golden rules:

**Contextualise the test.** Don't pull out a generic algorithm. Create a mini-project that resembles your real need. If you're looking for a freelancer to rebuild your back office, give them a mock-up and ask them to code a simple but well-architected CRUD feature.

**Limit the time.** Nobody takes a test that requires more than 3 hours seriously. The best freelancers already have missions; they're not going to spend a weekend on your exercise. Aim for 1.5 to 2 hours max.

**Pay for the test.** Yes, you read that right. Paying for a technical test (even symbolically, €100-200) sends a strong signal: you respect the freelancer's time and you're serious about the process. As a bonus, it filters out opportunistic candidates who apply in bulk without any real investment.

**Evaluate the approach as much as the result.** Perfect code delivered without explanation is worth nothing. Ask the freelancer to comment on their approach, their technical choices, what they would have done differently with more time. That's where the real added value shows.

### Step 4: Peer-review validation

Even if you have an in-house CTO, have the profile validated by another developer. Peer review avoids confirmation bias and brings a fresh eye. Concretely:

Your lead developer spends 30 minutes on a video call with the freelancer on a sharp technical topic. Not a formal interview — just a peer-to-peer exchange on a concrete problem. Technical chemistry verifies itself in real conditions.

The goal isn't to trap the candidate, but to see whether they can hold a high-level technical conversation, whether they know the state of the art of their technology, whether they have well-argued opinions on architecture choices.

If your internal team doesn't have the skills to validate a highly specialised profile (data science, blockchain, AI), bring in an external expert. It costs €200-300 for an hour-long validation, but it can save you from burning €50K on an incompetent freelancer.

## The signals that never lie

Beyond formal tests, certain behavioural signals are very good indicators of technical competence.

**The ability to say "I don't know".** A freelancer who claims to master everything is either dishonest or incompetent. The best profiles know how to acknowledge their limits and propose to skill up quickly.

**Technical curiosity.** During the interview, does the freelancer ask questions about your stack, your business constraints, your scalability stakes? Someone who doesn't try to understand your context will never deliver a relevant solution.

**Honesty about timelines.** Beware of freelancers who promise the moon in two weeks. A good profile gives you a realistic estimate, with safety margins and identified warning points.

**Clear communication.** A developer who can explain complex technical concepts in plain terms is someone who has genuinely understood what they're doing. If everything stays fuzzy and full of hollow jargon, that's a bad sign.

## The tools to speed up the process

You don't necessarily have the time or resources to build a full selection pipeline. Fortunately, some tools make technical evaluation easier:

**Coderbyte, HackerRank**: standardised technical testing platforms. Handy as a first filter, but watch out for profiles who train specifically for these exercises.

**GitLab / GitHub**: analyse the commit history, the contribution frequency, the quality of pull requests. An active open-source profile is generally a good indicator.

**LinkedIn Skill Assessments**: validated skill badges can give a quick hint, but never take them at face value.

**Stack Overflow / Dev.to**: a freelancer who shares knowledge and answers technical questions shows real expertise. Look up their handle on these platforms.

## The questions you absolutely must ask in the interview

Here's a checklist of questions I systematically use to evaluate a freelancer's skills:

"Walk me through your last complex project. What was the main challenge and how did you solve it?" It reveals analytical and problem-solving ability.

"What's the worst technical mistake you've made, and what did you learn from it?" The best freelancers have war scars and can talk about them without bullshit.

"How do you stay current on your technology?" A good profile follows blogs, attends conferences, contributes to open source. Someone who answers "I watch YouTube tutorials from time to time" isn't at senior level.

"If we gave you carte blanche on this project's architecture, what would you go with and why?" You're testing technical vision and the ability to justify strategic choices.

"How do you handle technical disagreements with a client or a team?" The relational dimension matters enormously in freelancing. A technical expert who can't communicate or negotiate will be counter-productive.

## FAQ: evaluating a freelancer's technical level

### How long does it take to properly evaluate a freelancer?

With a structured process, you can qualify a profile in 48 hours: portfolio and code review (1h), technical interview (1h), targeted test (2h), peer review (30 min). But don't drag your feet either: the best freelancers are in demand and don't stay available for long.

### Should even a senior profile take a technical test?

Yes, no exceptions. Seniority doesn't guarantee competence. I've seen profiles with 15 years of experience who had never evolved since their early days. A context-appropriate technical test remains the only reliable way to validate operational skills.

### Can certifications and degrees be trusted when choosing an IT freelancer?

Certifications (AWS, Google Cloud, Azure) prove a freelancer has validated a theoretical foundation. That's a good signal, but insufficient. An AWS Solutions Architect certification doesn't guarantee the person can design a scalable infrastructure suited to your business context. Use certifications as a pre-selection filter, never as a final criterion.

### How do I evaluate a freelancer on a technology we don't master in-house?

Three options: bring in an external expert to validate the profile (€300-500 for an evaluation session), use specialised networks that pre-qualify skills, or quickly get up to speed on the technology's fundamentals so you at least understand the vocabulary and the stakes. In any case, never choose blind.

### What's the difference between evaluating a junior and a senior freelancer?

For a junior, you mostly evaluate potential, learning ability and methodological rigour. From a senior, you expect autonomy, strategic advice, architectural vision and the ability to pass knowledge on. A senior's technical test should include questions on system architecture, scalability, technical debt and business trade-offs.
