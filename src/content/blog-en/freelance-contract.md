## Why large accounts favour the freelance model

The first reason is flexibility on budget and timing. Purchasing and finance departments appreciate being able to mobilise sharp expertise for a precise scope, over a defined period, without adding to payroll or committing to a permanent contract (CDI) when strategic uncertainty is high. For an innovation project, a seasonal e-commerce peak, a technical overhaul or an AI POC, the freelance contract stands out as an agile, measurable and reversible answer.

The second reason is access to rare talent. The best experts increasingly prefer independence, and are spread between solo freelancers and [**studios and collectives**](/en/studios-and-collectives). For a [**UX designer**](/en/roles/freelance-ux-designer) used to complex environments, a specialist AI data analyst or a [**freelance SEO consultant**](/en/roles/freelance-seo-consultant), a mission with a large account offers stimulating challenges, rich datasets and multidisciplinary teams. Companies gain seniority that is operational straight away, whereas an internal hire would take months to come through.

Finally, the third reason is external innovation. By exposing their teams to methods, tools and lessons learned from other sectors, large accounts shake up their silos and accelerate intrapreneurship. A fresh pair of eyes questions habits, challenges technical choices and sets delivery standards that rub off positively on the organisation.

## The 5 essential clauses of a corporate freelance contract

Start by clearly defining the purpose of the mission. Spell out the expected deliverables, the success indicators and the limits of the scope. One simple sentence — "Design and deliver a documented Figma design system covering templates X, Y and Z, with internal adoption > 80% by the end of Q2" — is worth more than three pages of generalities. This precision avoids misunderstandings, prevents scope creep and makes milestone sign-off easier.

Follow on with duration and schedule. State the start and end dates, sprint granularity, rituals (kick-off, demos, retros), as well as the conditions for postponement if something unexpected happens. Large accounts often work in budget cycles: anchor your milestones in this reality so the project stays a priority in internal governance.

Next, the financial terms must be explicit. Align the day rate, the payment schedule (deposit, monthly or milestone-based invoicing, payment terms), and how incidental expenses are handled (travel, licences, subcontracting). A balanced freelance contract protects both parties.

Take intellectual property seriously. Clarify the nature of the assignment of rights (full or partial), its extent (territory, duration, media), and when the transfer takes place (often upon full payment).

Finally, lock down confidentiality and GDPR. Large companies demand clauses close to those imposed on their own staff: trade secrets, information security, processing of personal data, any subcontracting. If you handle sensitive data, set out your plan for minimisation, pseudonymisation and purging in the contract itself. Compliance is prepared before the first CSV export, not after.

## Legal pitfalls to avoid on the freelancer's side

The first pitfall is economic dependence. If more than 70% of your revenue comes from a single client, you increase the risk of reclassification and, above all, you weaken your business if things change. Diversify your accounts or, failing that, build safeguards into the contract: a limited duration, explicit renewals, reasonable non-exclusivity clauses.

An abusive non-compete clause deserves a closer look. Banning any collaboration in an entire sector, worldwide, for two years, when the mission lasts three months, makes little sense. Negotiate a proportionate clause: a precise scope, a short duration and, if there is strong exclusivity, financial compensation. The law likes moderation.

The lack of a written contract is a third risk, and the most avoidable one. Without anything in writing, things are vague, and vagueness costs you in a dispute: delays, unpaid invoices, contested intellectual property. Even if you're starting quickly, at the very least formalise a signed purchase order, with scope, price, schedule, rights assigned and payment terms. Add an amendment if the scope changes: an email isn't always as good as a contract.

## FAQ – Your questions about freelance contracts

### What is the ideal length for a freelance contract with a large account?

To stay agile, go for a renewable arrangement of six to twelve months. That's long enough to deliver a significant increment — a redesign, a roll-out, a ramp-up — while allowing periodic reassessment of the scope, the day rate and the priorities. Large accounts like this format because it fits well into their annual and half-yearly budget cycles.

### Which status should you choose to work with a large company?

The choice depends on volume and clauses. The *micro-entreprise* (France's micro-business status) suits modest volumes and well-defined missions. Beyond a certain annual threshold, or where wider responsibilities are involved, a company (*SASU*/*EURL*, French single-shareholder company forms) or *portage salarial* (an umbrella company arrangement) offer more contractual latitude, insurability and credibility with purchasing departments. Portage, when required, smooths onboarding without depriving you of your operational autonomy.

### How do you avoid scope creep during a mission?

Prevention comes from meticulous initial scoping and steering rituals. At every demo, confirm what is "Done", log additional requests and propose an amendment or a backlog re-prioritisation. Once this reflex is in place, discussions become factual again: you adjust course rather than squabbling over interpretations.
