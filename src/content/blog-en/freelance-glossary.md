## Understanding freelancing without the jargon (or almost)

When a company or a start-up wants to move fast, it often turns to a freelancer. But who are they, really? A freelancer isn't an external salaried worker, nor a consultant in a three-piece suit there to impress the meeting: they're an independent, autonomous professional who makes their skills available for a set period.

Graphic designers, developers, consultants, acquisition specialists, content creators… freelancing covers an endless range of crafts.

And it often starts with a word you'll see everywhere: the _engagement_ (in French, the *mission*).

An engagement is the heart of the collaboration. It's your project: redoing a web page, launching a [social ads](/en/roles/freelance-social-ads-expert) campaign, creating a visual identity, structuring an SEO strategy. The freelancer commits to it, based on a level of expertise and an estimated amount of time.

At some point, you may come across the famous *TJM* (*Taux Journalier Moyen*, the average day rate). Not a secret code: just the rate per day of work. It reflects experience, speed of execution and the ability to manage a project autonomously.

## How does a collaboration really get started?

People often imagine that everything is decided the moment you say "OK, let's go". In reality, the magic mostly happens before the project starts. And it all begins with a **brief**, a document that's often underestimated but absolutely essential.

A good brief isn't a novel — it's a spotlight: what you want, why you want it, who it's for and by when. If you had to give the freelancer the treasure map, it would be the brief. The more precise it is, the better your chances of getting exactly what you had in mind, or even better.

Next comes the step many people underestimate: **onboarding**.

People often think it only concerns salaried staff, but think again: a freelancer also needs to be integrated, even briefly. Onboarding means giving them access to the right tools, introducing the key contacts, and clarifying the schedule, the sign-off process and the expected style.

Which brings us to another key word: **deliverables**. These are everything the freelancer will produce for you. An edited video, an SEO report, a user journey, an optimised article, a Figma mock-up…

The most important thing? That everyone agrees on what the final result should look like. Misunderstandings often happen when people assume "it goes without saying". Spoiler: it never goes without saying.

Then there are **deadlines**. A well-set deadline is realistic, discussed and sometimes broken down into stages, especially if the project is dense.

Finally, there's a word that can transform a collaboration: **feedback**.

One clear, precise, illustrated piece of feedback is worth a thousand vague messages.

The **quote** is the starting point: a commercial proposal from the freelancer setting out the scope of the engagement, the price, the terms and the deadlines. Signing it means approving the framework of the collaboration.

Then comes the **invoice**, which can be issued at the start, at the end or throughout the project, depending on what was agreed. Every invoice includes mandatory information — *SIRET* (French business registration number), VAT, description of the service — and marks the administrative reality of the engagement.

In some cases, you'll see the notion of a **deposit**. Between 20% and 50% of the total amount, it secures the time the freelancer devotes to your project. A healthy practice, especially for long engagements.

In other situations, particularly with large companies, you'll come across *portage salarial* (France's umbrella company model). It's an alternative that lets the freelancer keep their autonomy while benefiting from a structure that handles all the admin. For you, the client, it's seamless: same collaboration, same results, more compliance.
